## Overview

The aurora borealis and aurora australis are natural light displays appearing in Earth's upper atmosphere at high latitudes, produced when charged particles from the Sun collide with atmospheric gases, exciting oxygen and nitrogen molecules to emit green, red, blue, and purple light. The scientific explanation was progressively developed by researchers including Anders Celsius, Edmond Halley, and Benjamin Franklin, with the electromagnetic mechanism established in the 19th and early 20th centuries. Before and alongside this scientific understanding, virtually every culture with exposure to the aurora developed rich supernatural interpretations. Sami peoples of northern Scandinavia believed the aurora was created by the souls of the dead dancing across the sky and considered it dangerous to attract the lights' attention by whistling, singing, or waving. Norse traditions variously interpreted the aurora as the breath of brave fallen warriors, the reflections of the Valkyries' shields as they rode across the sky, or the Bifrost bridge connecting the mortal world to Asgard. Maori traditions described the aurora australis as 'Tahunui-a-rangi' (great torches in the sky) created by ancestors who voyaged to southern ice lands around the 7th century. Aboriginal Australian communities associated the aurora australis with fire and spirit beings. Finnish tradition gave rise to the legend of the 'revontulet' (fox fires), imagining a cosmic fox running across the sky, its tail sweeping sparks across the darkness. Inuit and other Arctic peoples often interpreted the aurora as the spirits of animals or deceased ancestors. The Norwegian chronicle Konungs Skuggsjá (circa 1230) records multiple competing Norse explanations, indicating the aurora was already a subject of active philosophical debate in medieval Scandinavia.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: 'Aurora borealis' combines Aurora (Roman goddess of dawn) with Boreas (Greek god of the north wind). The phrase was applied to the northern lights by Pierre Gassendi following an aurora he observed in 1621, and the term entered widespread English use by the 18th century. 'Aurora australis' follows the same pattern with Auster (Latin, south wind). The English colloquial 'northern lights' predates the Latin scholarly terminology.
- **Meaning**: Aurora: goddess of dawn; Borealis: of the north; Australis: of the south. Together: northern/southern dawn.
- **First use**: 1621 (Pierre Gassendi's application of 'Aurora Borealis'); English use from the 18th century onward

## Distribution

Aurora borealis occurs in a ring around the Arctic (auroral oval), centered on the geomagnetic pole. During strong geomagnetic storms it can be seen as far south as the northern United States, central Europe, and occasionally further. Aurora australis similarly occurs around the Antarctic. Both appear year-round but require darkness and clear skies to be visible.

## Theories & Explanations

### Paranormal / Cryptozoological

**Souls of the dead (Sami tradition)** _(Sami oral tradition)_

Sami peoples of northern Scandinavia traditionally understood the aurora as the souls of the dead dancing across the sky. It was considered dangerous to attract the attention of the spirits by whistling, singing, or waving at the lights. The Sami word for aurora, 'guovssahas', is sometimes translated as 'the light you can hear', reflecting a sensory tradition of listening to as well as watching the lights.

**Valkyries' shields / Bifrost bridge (Norse tradition)** _(Norse mythology / Old Norse literature)_

Norse traditions variously interpreted the aurora as the reflections of the Valkyries' shields and armor as these warrior spirits rode across the sky conducting fallen heroes to Valhalla, or as the Bifrost — the shimmering rainbow bridge connecting the mortal world to Asgard. Some accounts identified the aurora as the breath of warriors who died bravely in combat.

**Ancestor spirits and animal spirits (Inuit / Arctic traditions)** _(Inuit oral traditions, Yupik oral traditions)_

Various Inuit and Arctic peoples understood the aurora as the spirits of deceased ancestors or the spirits of animals — seals, walrus, deer — that humans depend on. The lights represented the continuation of life and relationship between the living and the dead.

### Scientific

**Solar wind electromagnetic interaction** _(Mainstream atmospheric physics and space science)_

The aurora is produced when charged particles (primarily electrons and protons) emitted by the Sun in the solar wind are captured by Earth's magnetic field and channeled into the polar regions, where they collide with and excite atmospheric gas molecules (oxygen and nitrogen). The molecules emit photons as they return to their ground state, producing the visible aurora. Green light comes from oxygen at approximately 100 km altitude; red from oxygen above 200 km; blue and violet from nitrogen.


## Indigenous Perspectives

> ⚠️ **Aurora traditions of Arctic indigenous peoples are part of living cultural and spiritual systems. Do not reduce these traditions to tourist-facing 'legends' or treat them as quaint superstitions contrasted with 'real' science.**

**Cultures**: Sami, Inuit, Yupik, Aleut, First Nations of Canada (various), Maori, Aboriginal Australians (various groups)

Aurora phenomena hold deep spiritual significance for many Arctic and sub-polar indigenous peoples. Sami aurora traditions are integrated with broader cosmological beliefs about life, death, and the spirit world. Inuit and Yupik traditions connect aurora to ancestral relationships and animal kinship. These are living traditions deserving of respectful engagement, not historical curiosities.

_Guidelines: Engage with indigenous sources directly when possible. Credit specific communities rather than speaking of 'Eskimo' or undifferentiated 'Native' belief. Acknowledge that scientific and indigenous understandings can coexist respectfully._
